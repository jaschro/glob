The spreadsheet-style “recipe summary table” invented in June 2004 by Silicon Valley engineer Michael Chu for his website, Cooking for Engineers. Unlike traditional recipe layouts, the spreadsheet-like cards line up ingredients and methods in overlapping steps. Designed like a Gantt chart or a dependency graph, the table lists ingredients down the left column, while each column to the right represents a culinary operation where cells merge as components are combined. This structural layout completely solves the cognitive load of cooking from traditional prose, giving the home cook a literal physical location on the screen that map out exact chronological dependencies and simultaneous actions at a single glance.

The innovative layout recently exploded in popularity on social media platforms like X, Threads, and Reddit, with high-profile culinary creators praising it as vastly superior to standard modern cookbooks. However, the broader food media industry has largely ignored this highly functional design for over two decades due to the unique economics of online publishing and copyright law. Under U.S. copyright law, simple lists of ingredients and bare instructions are considered unprotectable facts, meaning that the long, narrative storytelling and personal anecdotes typical of modern recipe blogs are the only elements the law legally protects and that algorithms reward for advertising revenue.
